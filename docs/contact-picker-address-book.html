One Contact Is Different From an Address Book

Contact permission has long been presented as a single choice. An app asks for the address book, and the user either grants access to everyone or gives up a convenient friend-finding feature.

Google’s 2026 Android security overview describes a more precise option in Android 17. A new contact picker lets a person choose specific contacts. An app can also request only the fields it needs, and the access can be temporary. Google says this is intended to reduce the broad address-book access apps often request just to connect one person with one friend.

That distinction is especially useful for messaging apps. A contact list can contain private phone numbers, family relationships, doctors, clients, and people who never agreed to share their details with another service. Selecting one person for an invitation has a much smaller privacy cost than handing over the full list.

The picker does not settle every privacy question. An app may still receive the contact data a user selects, and older Android versions may use broader permission models. People should read the prompt, check which fields are requested, and deny access when the feature does not need it. Typing a username manually can be slower and still be the better choice for a sensitive contact.

This also clarifies the word anonymous. A public profile can hide a legal name while contact syncing reveals a rich network of real-world relationships.
