# A Secure Messenger Still Needs a High Risk Mode

WhatsApp’s response to recent spyware activity makes a useful point about messaging security. End-to-end encryption protects message content in transit and storage, but a targeted attack may arrive through an attachment, an unknown call, a malicious link, or the phone itself.

In a June 2026 update, WhatsApp said it had disrupted spear-phishing attempts linked to NSO and published malicious-domain indicators for researchers and defenders. The company again recommended Strict Account Settings for people who believe they may face sophisticated attacks. That optional mode blocks attachments and media from unknown senders, silences calls from unknown people, and tightens other settings. The restrictions may reduce convenience, which is exactly why they are not the default for everyone.

This gives ordinary users a better comparison test than asking which app has the strongest slogan. Look at the product’s default encryption, then look at what happens around the encrypted channel. Can unknown people call you? Are message requests encrypted before acceptance? What metadata remains visible? Can an AI feature receive content from the conversation? Is there a lockdown mode for a temporary period of higher risk?

Risk also changes over time. Someone covering an election, negotiating a disputed deal, or responding to online harassment may need stricter settings for a month and normal settings later. A messenger that makes those controls understandable has an advantage even when two products use strong cryptography.
